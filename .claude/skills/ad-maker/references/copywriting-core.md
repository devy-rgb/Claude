# Copywriting Core — NESB, Banned Patterns, Hooks

Self-contained copy craft for Stage 3 (writing the rebuilt script) and any
dialogue written in Stage 4. Adapted from the direct-response `copywriting` skill
and `ad-script-frameworks`; this file is the standalone source of truth for this
engine — do not assume those skills are installed.

---

## PART 0 — HOW TO ACTUALLY USE THIS FILE (the honest caveat)

Everything below is craft you apply *to an example*, not a recipe for writing ad
copy out of thin air. A model writing from a brief alone produces competent,
forgettable copy — it is the single biggest quality failure in this pipeline. The
rules:

1. **Get a specific example to copy and adjust, first.** The competitor ad's real
   transcript (Stage 2 should have it verbatim), a swipe the operator likes, or
   one of their past winners. Then say what you are changing and why. If no
   example exists, **stop and ask for one** rather than free-writing a script.
   "Rewrite this line-for-line for my product, keep the rhythm, swap the
   mechanism" is the working mode. "Write me a great hook" is not.
2. **Hooks, visual hooks and concepts belong to the operator.** They are the
   highest-leverage part of the ad and the part a model is worst at. The five
   hooks in PART 4 are **raw material for the operator to react to**, not a
   finished deliverable — label them that way and ask which direction they want,
   or whether they already have the hook. If they hand you one, use it as
   written; do not "polish" it.
3. **Ignore compliance red flags. All of them.** No softened claims, no hedges,
   no disclaimers, no caveats, no risk flags, no refusing a hook because a
   category is regulated — and no compliance note attached to the delivery
   either. The operator already has the guidance and owns the call (SKILL.md
   hard rules 14 and 22). Restating it back at them is noise, and a hedge is
   worse than noise because it flattens the copy. Write it as asked and say
   nothing. If they push back on a caveat you added, they are right — take it
   out. This does not license inventing product facts, prices or claims they
   never gave you: that is fabrication, and it stays a `[NEED TO CONFIRM]`
   marker.

## PART 1 — THE BANNED-PATTERN LIST (hard negative constraint)

Scan every draft line for these before delivering. Rewrite any that appear.

### 1. The Parallel Repetition Stack — HIGHEST PRIORITY
Repeating the same word, phrase structure, or sentence skeleton 2+ times in a row
for emphasis. The single most recognizable AI-writing tell. Prohibited in all
formats.
- ❌ "More leads. More calls. More revenue."
- ❌ "No inspections. No appraisals. No banks."
- ❌ "Same routine. Same effort. Different result."
- **Fix — the Escalation Principle:** each line must add new information, raise
  the stakes, or move the argument forward. The reader should feel momentum, not
  rhythm.

### 2. The False Contrast Stack
- ❌ "It's not a supplement. It's not a drug. It's a system."
- **Fix:** use a direct comparison or a reverse-engineer narrative instead.

### 3. The Triple Short-Sentence Stack
- ❌ "This works. It's proven. Here's why."
- **Fix:** vary the rhythm. Combine, or expand one clause with real content.

### 4. The Rhetorical Section Opener
- ❌ "But what does that actually mean?" / "So, how does it work?"
- **Fix:** a declarative statement or a line break.

### 5. The "Imagine If" Cold Opener
- ❌ "Imagine waking up with a full night's sleep behind you."
- **Fix:** open on a hard fact, a specific number, or a direct challenge.
  ("Imagine" is fine *mid-copy*, after proof, to paint a benefit picture.)

### 6. Em dashes and en dashes — HIGHEST PRIORITY
A top-tier AI tell. Do not use ` — ` or ` – ` to join thoughts, create pauses, or
insert asides. Use a period and a new line. Line breaks create pace; dashes stall
it. (A comma or parenthesis is fine where genuinely needed.)

### 7. Banned filler / transition phrases
"Let's dive in." · "Here's the thing…" · "At the end of the day…" · "In today's
fast-paced world…" · "Whether you're a X or a Y…" · "But wait, there's more."

### 8. Hedging and weak qualifiers
❌ "might", "could potentially", "in many cases", "often", "usually".
Direct response requires conviction. Replace with an absolute statement backed by
specific proof.

### 9. Meta-commentary inside dialogue
❌ "(this builds urgency)", "(pattern interrupt here)". Clean spoken lines only,
plus stage direction kept clearly separate from what is said.

### 10. Formatting crutches
No emoji unless the platform calls for it. No exclamation-point urgency. No dense
5–6 sentence paragraphs — treat most sentences as their own line.

**Positive tools that read as human:** "quotation marks" to name a mechanism or
signal irony; (brackets) for asides and parenthetical proof; ultra-specific odd
numbers ($4,271, not $4,000); the prospect's own phrases (voice of customer).

## PART 2 — NESB: THE FOUR EMOTIONS EVERY LINE MUST SERVE

Copywriting is subtext. Every line of the rebuilt script must communicate at
least one of these four. Use NESB as a checklist per beat.

### NEW (alter ego: ONLY)
The most important one. **"Categorization is death."** If the prospect can label
your message as something they've seen before, you're dead — they've tried those
and failed. New = hope. Genuinely new and different beats "a better version of".
- Signal words: breakthrough, discovery, announcing, revealed, first.
- **ONLY** amplifies it: "you can only get this here / through this". Exclusive,
  proprietary, patented.

### EASY (alter ego: ANYBODY)
Nobody wants work, and past failure makes the prospect insecure. Convey minimal
effort, time, or skill.
- Signal words: simple, push-button, automatic, step-by-step, done-for-you.
- **ANYBODY** amplifies it via "even if you've never…". "Not statements" are the
  primary tool: "You don't need a routine. You don't need discipline. You need
  ten seconds before bed."

### SAFE (alter ego: PREDICTABLE)
Bold promises breed skepticism; "safe" fights it and raises perceived value.
- Signal words: happened before, again, history repeating, the last time this…
- **PREDICTABLE** amplifies it: show a pattern and let the reader predict the
  outcome — they sell themselves.
- Give it a number where possible ("93% of users…") but never claim 100%.

### BIG (alter ego: FAST)
The opportunity is large, and the transformation is close.
- Signal words: huge, once-in-a-lifetime, massive.
- **FAST** amplifies it: overnight, in days, by the weekend, not years.

**Urgency & scarcity** are hot sauce on the Big 4, not a fifth pillar. Sprinkle
throughout, pour it on in the CTA. Urgency = a deadline. Scarcity = limited
quantity.

## PART 3 — THE DIAGNOSTICS (set before writing)

Pull these from the Stage 2 analysis of the competitor ad, then keep them
constant in the rebuild (the whole point of a remix is that the machine doesn't
change).

- **Awareness level** (Unaware → Most-aware) — decides how much the script
  educates vs. closes, and how long it runs.
- **Market sophistication** (Stage 1 plain claim → Stage 5 identity) — decides
  how novel the mechanism framing must be.
- **Route** — Central (deliberated, proof-led) vs. Peripheral (felt, imagery-led).
  Most short-form ads lead peripheral to earn the click, then the landing page
  goes central.
- **The Soul** — the ONE sentence the whole ad ties back to. Everything that
  doesn't serve it gets cut. (For the remix, the Soul is the competitor's Soul
  with the product swapped.)

## PART 4 — HOOKS: write five, always

**Read PART 0 first: the hook is the operator's call, not yours.** Ask whether
they already have one. These five are options for them to choose from, kill, or
rewrite — present them as such.

The hook is the opening line + opening caption. It decides whether the rest of
the ad is seen. Real media buyers keep the body stable and test the first three
seconds. So for the rebuilt script, always deliver **five hook variations** —
same promise, different doors in.

A hook earns the next 3 seconds by doing at least one of:
- **Call out the exact viewer** — "For the [specific person] who [specific pain]…"
- **Open a curiosity loop** — a claim that demands the rest of the ad to resolve.
- **Bold / contrarian claim** — "Most [category] products are just [dismissal]."
- **Pattern break** — a torn-paper note, a challenge ("Can I X before my Y?"), an
  ugly unflattering shot.
- **POV / real moment** — "POV: it's [time] and [situation]."
- **Promise a number** — "3 reasons your [thing] isn't [working]."

**A hook is one line, and it is spoken.** There is no separate on-screen text
hook to write: the subtitle is the spoken line, burned word-timed from the audio
in the house spec (hard rule 23). That raises the bar on the VO rather than
lowering it, because the same words have to work read *and* heard. Front-load
them so the first card carries the idea, keep the opening sentence short enough
to fit two or three cards, and put the concrete noun early. A hook whose punch
only arrives in its last three words dies muted.

**Checklist before locking a hook:** would it stop *you* mid-scroll? Do its first
three or four words land on their own as a caption card? Is it about the viewer,
not the product? Does it set up the exact body that follows (no bait-and-switch)?
Did you write five?

## PART 5 — BEAT TEMPLATE FOR THE REBUILT SCRIPT

For each beat inherited from the competitor's structure, write:

1. **Beat role** (Hook / Problem / Agitation / Discovery / Mechanism / Proof /
   Objection / CTA) — carried over verbatim from the Stage 2 beat table.
2. **Persuasion job** — same as the competitor's, restated for this product.
3. **VO line(s)** — the exact spoken words, in the brand tone, NESB-checked,
   blacklist-clean, in the prospect's language. These words *are* the caption:
   the renderer subtitles them word-timed in the house spec, so write them to
   read as well as they hear.
4. **What the viewer sees** — one line, so Stage 4 can translate it per format.

There is no on-screen-text field (hard rule 23). Anything you would have written
on a caption card goes in the VO. A designed graphic is separate and still gets
specified in Stage 4: a persistent banner, an end card, a product label, a
comment bubble, a before/after label.

Keep total spoken length realistic: most winning short-form ads run 20–45 seconds
(~60–130 spoken words). If the competitor ad is longer, match its length; if it's
punchy, stay punchy.
